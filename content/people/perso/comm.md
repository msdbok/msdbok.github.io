# Communication

Communication in a project is the deliberate part of getting a message across: what you say, how
you deliver it, and through which channel. All three are choices, and the same content can build a
team or damage it depending on how they are made.

## 1. Constructive or destructive is a choice

Constructive communication builds morale, teamwork and relationships; destructive communication
produces conflict, resentment and resistance {% cite arredondo2000communicating %}. The content is
often identical — only the delivery changed. Two things separate them. **Approach** is choosing the
when and the where, and it is the first thing dropped in a busy week. **Building bridges** is naming
the behaviour rather than the person, and finding the common ground that the disagreement sits on.

For example, "your migration broke production" said in front of nine people at standup, and the
same fact raised ten minutes later with the two people who can fix it, carry identical information
and produce opposite weeks. Decide where and when before a hard conversation; that is half the
decision already made.

## 2. Listening is a technique, not an attitude

Listening fails in predictable ways, and none of them is a lack of goodwill. It fails when you
listen only for facts and miss the concern underneath them; when you compose your reply while the
other person is still talking; when you finish their sentences. Thought runs far faster than
speech, and the gap fills with your own argument unless you give it something to do.

Giving it something to do is the technique. **Paraphrasing** is the one move that shows: say back,
in your own words, what you think was said, and let the other person correct it. Around that,
circulate meeting notes and invite corrections, ask questions that are not challenges, and signal
that you are still there — nods, eye contact, and on a call, the camera. Ask, look, listen, and
then answer. In this course the payoff is immediate: paraphrasing is the technique a case-discussion
room notices.

## 3. Meeting intent decides onsite or remote

The old question was how far apart people sit. The current one is which meetings need a room. In a
study of 27 interviews across all 15 agile teams at one Ericsson site, **the intent of the meeting
decided the useful mode**: retrospectives, sprint and user-story planning and technical discussions
benefited from being onsite, while large information-sharing meetings worked well — sometimes
better — remotely {% cite christensen2025hybrid %}. The same study warns against filling the office
days with meetings, because back-to-back sessions destroy the informal conversation that was the
reason to come in.

Distance itself matters less than it appears to. Across a review of 255 studies, **perceived**
distance predicted collaboration where spatio-temporal distance did not
{% cite morrison2020virtual %}: without casual encounters, teams substitute assumptions about each
other's effort for observation of it, and those assumptions skew negative.

A model in the conversation is not a neutral pipe either. Collective attention drifts toward
whatever an AI participant has just named, even though most participants deny it is part of the
team at all — influence without membership, which no media-richness account of a channel predicts
{% cite riedl2024spillover %}.

## How solid is this?

- **The constructive/destructive material is not held.** Arredondo's book is in the bibliography
  but no copy is available here, so the contrast and the approach/build-bridges structure come
  through the Fall 2025 lecture deck. No page reference is given, and none should be invented.
- **The listening material has no citation at all, deliberately.** It traces to Harris and
  Sherblom's *Small Group and Team Communication*, which this course does not hold and which has no
  bibliography key. Nothing in the reviewed corpus evaluates an active-listening technique. It is
  kept as practitioner teaching material because the case presentations need it.
- **The hybrid-meeting finding is contested, not settled.** Christensen and colleagues studied one
  company at one Finnish site, exploratory and explicitly not causal, and their retrospectives
  result contradicts prior work that found retrospectives held online for inclusion.
- **The virtual-teams review is narrative, not systematic**: no quality appraisal, no effect sizes,
  and a corpus that predates 2019 and so predates mass remote work.
- **The AI result is small and not about software.** Undergraduate laboratory teams on a puzzle
  task; in one condition the "AI" was pre-recorded audio; the flagship effect is roughly one extra
  word per twenty-word utterance, and the authors state it establishes no base rate for real
  settings.
